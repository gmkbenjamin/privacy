Latch Privacy Policy

Effective 7 November 2026 · Part of the SmartStay Access privacy policy

Latch is a personal utility that unlocks your own Mac from your own iPhone or iPad. This policy covers the Latch iOS app.

Summary

Latch collects nothing. There is no account, no analytics, no advertising, no tracking, and no third-party SDK of any kind. The app talks only to Macs you have explicitly paired with, and nothing is sent to the developer or to any other server.

Data we collect

None. The developer receives no data from this app — not usage data, not diagnostics, not crash reports, not identifiers.

All of the following stays on your iPhone or iPad. Latch never uploads it automatically.

Proximity reports to your Mac from the background, usually while your phone is locked, and every report is signed with the pairing secret. While it is on, the two Keychain items above are therefore readable once the phone has been unlocked after starting up, rather than only while it is unlocked. Turning Proximity off puts them back — once the Mac has been told it is off. If it can't be told then, the app waits for the Mac to come into Bluetooth range and tells it, and the items stay readable until it has.

The device identifier is a random value created locally by Latch. It is not the IDFA, not the IDFV, and is not used for advertising, tracking, or identification across apps. ThisDeviceOnly means neither Keychain item ever moves to another device, even through a backup.

Deleting the app removes your settings but not the Keychain items: iOS keeps an app's Keychain items after the app is deleted. Your pairing records and device identifier therefore stay on the device, and reinstalling Latch picks them up again. To remove the pairing records, remove your Macs in the app before deleting it — touch and hold a Mac and choose Remove pairing.

Authenticator associations and Mac access

Saved authenticator accounts can include website domains you enter for AutoFill suggestions. Those associations stay with the protected account vault, synchronize directly to paired Macs with authenticator access, and are included in encrypted backups you choose to create. Enabling AutoFill shares credential identities with the operating system; Latch requires device-owner authentication before providing a code.

Device permissions and the Secure Mac automatic-unlock hold are stored in the Mac’s Keychain. The phone receives its current permissions and each reachable Mac’s confirmed state. Removing permission prevents future requests; it does not remotely erase accounts or captures already saved on the phone.

Optional Mac automation rules stay on that Mac. They contain the event, Shortcut name or script path, literal arguments and timeout you choose. Latch records recent result metadata, not script output. Shortcuts and scripts run with your Mac user’s access and may perform network or other actions you have configured in them. These user-selected actions are separate from Latch’s direct paired-device communications.

Optional local diagnostics

Settings → Diagnostics on iPhone, iPad and Mac can record troubleshooting logs. This is off by default. Logs include connection and handshake stages, request timing, signal strength and proximity policy flags, lock-event times and causes, notification scheduling outcomes and available OS delivery records, and widget save/reload requests. They exclude passwords, pairing secrets, device names, network addresses, photos, videos, message payloads, typed text and pointer positions. Device aliases use a private random salt that changes each launch; random request IDs let you compare the same exchange across your devices.

The app keeps up to 8 MiB of rolling logs in its own Application Support storage, plus one export copy of up to 8 MiB, excluded from backups. On iOS these files are protected until the first unlock after restarting, then remain writable for background diagnostics. Older entries are replaced; recording does not keep the phone or Mac awake. Turning recording off stops new entries, and Clear saved logs removes the rolling files and local export. Copies you have shared must be removed separately.

Logs stay on the device unless you choose Prepare logs for sharing and share the export. They can reveal times of activity and near/away states, so review them before sharing. The developer receives no diagnostic data automatically. Recording does not change your lock/unlock rules or adapt them automatically.

Live camera and microphone

Camera & Mic is an optional, foreground live view from your paired Mac to this iPhone or iPad. The iPhone asks for Face ID, Touch ID or its passcode before starting; macOS separately requires Camera and Microphone permission. Video and audio use a dedicated authenticated encrypted network connection. They stay in bounded memory buffers and are not recorded, added to History, uploaded to a cloud service or sent to the developer. The iPhone only plays the Mac audio; this feature does not use the iPhone microphone.

Close live view, leave the iPhone app, or use Stop on either device to end it. If the connection is lost, a continuous-time lease stops capture within 12 seconds, plus device cleanup. System sleep, pairing revocation and security captures also stop live view. Security photos and silent clips remain separate, opt-in features described below.

Data sent off your device

Latch sends a pairing request to the Mac you are pairing with, and unlock and lock requests, requests to turn its displays off, sleep it, mute it, play or pause, or play its alert sound, requests for photos or silent videos it took, status queries and, while Proximity is on, whether your phone is near, its Bluetooth signal strength, how long ago it last read as far away, whether it counted your steps on the way, whether it is on a charger, whether it is in your hand (when you ask for that), whether it walked off while out of touch with the Mac, whether it is locked, your Proximity settings, and with each lock or unlock request where in the app it came from only to the Macs you paired, over your local network, Bluetooth LE, or a VPN or private network you run yourself. There is no developer server and no cloud component.

When a paired Mac can't be found, the app may send a Wake-on-LAN packet to the addresses that Mac last had on your network — only while your phone is on that same network. The packet carries only the Mac's own hardware address, which wakes it.

That traffic is end-to-end encrypted between your devices using Curve25519 ECDH key agreement, HKDF, and AES-GCM, and each request is HMAC-signed with the pairing secret established when you paired.

Each request tells the Mac which device sent it, so the Mac can list who unlocked it: the device identifier, and a label such as “iPhone · 3F2A” — the model name iOS reports, plus a tag taken from the identifier. The identifier is the one exception to the encryption: it is also sent unencrypted at the start of each connection, so other devices on the same network or within Bluetooth range can see it. It is random and reveals nothing about you.

Your Mac login password

Latch on the Mac can store your macOS login password so it can type it at the lock screen. The saved password is stored on the Mac only and is never sent back to the iOS app. During normal unlocking, the iOS app sends a request meaning "unlock"; the Mac supplies its own password locally. In Mac Settings on iPhone, you may enter a replacement after authenticating on the iPhone. That replacement is held temporarily in memory, sent only to the selected paired Mac over the encrypted authenticated connection, and saved there. It is not saved in iPhone preferences, history or diagnostic logs, and the field is cleared when saved or when leaving the password screen. This changes Latch's saved copy, not your macOS account password. Latch types it only into the lock screen's masked password field, and nowhere else.

Latch on the Mac also keeps a short history of its locks and unlocks, and of wrong passwords typed at its lock screen, on the Mac, so your paired phones can show it. To tell a password from Touch ID, a lock by hand from one the Mac made itself, and to notice a wrong password, it reads the lock screen's own entries in the Mac's system log — while the screen is locked, it watches for the wrong password entry. After a rejected password, it also reads a short, filtered window of system power-activity entries to recognise a matching Codex Computer Use interval. Only the likely source category is kept with the history and sent to paired phones. It is labelled as a correlation, and other attempts show source unknown. Raw log entries, process IDs, password contents and typed text are not kept or sent.

Latch on the Mac

The iOS Mac Settings screen reads and changes preferences saved in Latch on the selected paired Mac, including capture, proximity, startup and password storage settings. Changes use the same encrypted authenticated connection as unlocking. You may also upload a custom alert audio file of up to 256 KB; the Mac validates and stores that file locally. macOS permission approvals must be completed on the Mac. Disabling unlock requests keeps only paired settings and status available over Bluetooth, so you can enable requests again from the iPhone; lock, unlock, actions and proximity automation remain disabled.

The Mac app keeps everything it stores on the Mac, and sends it only to your paired phones:

  • Unlock photos and silent videos. Mac Settings has separate controls for failed attempts and successful password unlocks, with independent photo, 3/5/10-second video, away-only, photo-delivery and video-delivery switches. New options are off; an existing failed-attempt photo choice is preserved. Successful password capture excludes Latch, Touch ID and Apple Watch unlocks. Enabling capture requests camera permission. The camera light comes on, and no microphone or audio is recorded. The Mac keeps the latest 20 captured events in a private local folder. Photo files are limited to 512 KiB and video files to 2 MiB. Only media captured with delivery enabled, and still permitted by the current delivery setting, can be fetched by paired phones over their encrypted authenticated connection. Local-only captures can be opened from Settings in Finder. Removing the last pairing or resetting pairings deletes all Mac captures.
  • Notification media copies. iOS makes a separate photo or video copy for the operating system when that attachment is enabled. These copies remain readable after the phone's first unlock following a restart, so alerts can display them while locked. A bounded in-memory cache holds recently received media for this purpose. Notification attachments are managed by iOS and can remain in Notification Center until you dismiss the notification.
  • Pairing secrets are kept in a file encrypted with a key held in your login keychain; your login password, in the keychain or in a file encrypted the same way.
  • For proximity, it stores each paired phone's Bluetooth identifier, and the routers of networks you told it not to lock on. While a phone's link is down it listens for that phone's own Bluetooth broadcasts — to lock once the phone has gone, or to hold a lock off for a few minutes while it is still on the desk. Never to unlock. It writes each proximity report (near or away, signal strength, whether the phone is on a charger) to the Mac's system log, which macOS keeps for a few days.
  • It advertises a stable identifier — in its Bluetooth name and on the local network — so your phones can find it. Anyone nearby can see that a Latch Mac is there.

Tracking

Latch does not track you. It does not collect data for advertising, does not share data with data brokers, and contains no tracking domains. The bundled Apple privacy manifest declares no collection and no tracking.

Children

Latch is not directed at children and collects no data from anyone.

Your rights

Because no data ever reaches the developer, there is nothing for us to access, export, correct, or delete on your behalf. All data lives on your own devices, and Data stored on your device explains how to remove it. This is also why Latch does not sell or share personal information under the CCPA/CPRA, and why there is no controller-held personal data under the GDPR.
